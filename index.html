Locating the source of a mysterious beep with Claude

Our house has a box room, full of boxes that we still haven't unpacked from our last move. A couple of weeks ago something in that room started intermittently beeping. The beeps were far enough apart that standing in the room and trying to determine the source of the noise was frustrating, and I really didn't want to dig through every one of those boxes. So I had Claude Code build Beep Locator, a software system for triangulating the source of a mysterious beep using multiple devices scattered around the room. Here's what Beep Locator looks like after it has been running for a while: You run Python web server to show the primary UI, and then use the QR code or URL to connect as many devices as you can spare to act as listeners - I was using two iPads, a phone, a MacBook Pro and a Surface Ultra. The devices then play noises to each other to help estimate their locations. This didn't work particularly well, so I added a "Rough mode" where you can drag their icons around in the space to provide rough locations which are then adjusted based on those sounds. Once that's set up, the devices all listen for the beep! I added a clapping mode too so I could test it by clapping my hands in different locations around the room - displayed as diamonds on the map. This helped me gain confidence that it was capable of locating a sound. Beeps show up on the map as an X, plus a line in the "Sounds located" log. You can replay these sounds (from each device in turn) to confirm they are the mysterious beep you are looking for, then click "This is the mystery beep" to mark them as such. Once you've confirmed the mysterious beep frequency the system will listen out for it, measure the interval between beeps, and use the triangulated locations to lock down the most likely location on the map. The system worked as intended. It gave me the position of the beep to within about 40cm, then I dug through that specific pile of boxes and found an ancient carbon monoxide detector that desperately wanted its batteries replaced. How it works When I started working through this problem with Claude it quickly dug up BeepBeep: A High-Accuracy Acoustic-Based System for Ranging and Localization Using COTS Devices, a 2007 paper by Chunyi Peng, Guobin Shen, Yongguang Zhang, Yanlin Li, and Kun Tan. Here's that paper's abstract: We present the design, implementation, and evaluation of BeepBeep, a high-accuracy acoustic-based ranging system. It operates in a spontaneous, ad-hoc, and device-to-device context without leveraging any pre-planned infrastructure. It is a pure software-based solution and uses only the most basic set of commodity hardware – a speaker, a microphone, and some form of device-to-device communication – so that it is readily applicable to many low-cost sensor platforms and to most commercial-off-the-shelf mobile devices like cell phones and PDAs. It achieves high accuracy through a combination of three techniques: two-way sensing, self-recording, and sample counting. That sounded exactly like what we needed to build! Beep Locator runs a Python web application server that does most of the work. The listening devices then connect to that server via WebSockets and present a UI which requests browser microphone access. The listeners mainly work by recording a 60 second buffer of recent audio. They also run a simple beep detection algorithm (see detector.js), which uses Fast Fourier Transform and a high-pass filter to identify potential beeps. It sends these events to the main server. When a beep is reported, the server has a device play a short chirp (the device with the lowest round-trip time to the server), then asks every listener upload a clip from about 0.6s before the beep to 0.8s after that chirp. Qwen 3.8 27B on my laptop can interpret model weights, too

Yesterday's hot paper was Sparks of Weight-Space Programming: LLMs can natively read & write neural network weights by Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang, and Omar Khattab. The paper found that frontier models, in particular GPT-6 Astra, can both interpret and modify the raw weights of a machine learning model - literally a text description of the model containing a bunch of floating point numbers. I've been playing with one of the examples from the paper and I'm delighted to share that Qwen 3.8 27B running on my laptop can do this as well! Weight-Space Programming Here's the highlight from their abstract, describing impressive capabilities for GPT-6 Astra and coining the term Weight-Space Programming for when LLMs analyze and modify model weights: Just by looking at the raw weights, GPT-6 Astra can tell what a network was trained to do. And without any environment feedback, it has no trouble writing, from scratch, small game-playing networks that outplay networks trained with PPO for 100 million steps. Likewise, asked to make Qwen3.5-0.8B better at math without gradients, Astra identifies an issue in its weights causing reasoning loops that Qwen’s own model card warns about, and fixes this by natively editing 0.016% of the weights. With the same sampling settings, this weight correction raises MATH-500 from 7.9% to 48.1% and generalizes out of the box to instruction following, raising IFEval by 3x. All of this is possible without allowing Astra to write training loops. It uses its reasoning to figure out what each part of a neural network computes, or should compute, and changes the weights accordingly. We call this Weight-Space Programming. The paper combines two topics that I think deserve to be treated separately. The first is what models can do with raw weights without running tools or executing code. The second explores how well models, once armed with code, can run operations to update and improve other models. spaCy author Matthew Honnibal called the paper misleading for conflating the two, and Sean Goedecke deconstructed the Qwen3.5-0.8B update example as taking the same steps that a human ML researcher would use. I'm going to focus on the no-tools question, which I think is the more interesting of the two. The paper presents the first question as Can LLMs understand model behaviors from weights? They trained four neural networks to play simplified 10x10 grid versions of four classic computer games - Breakout, Pong, Pac-Man, and Freeway (I had to look that one up, it's an Atari 2600 game from 1981). Then they fed those weights (as floating point numbers) to an LLM along with the following question: The weights below belong to one policy network that was trained with reinforcement learning to play a game on this grid. Which game was it trained on? Work from the weights. You have no tools: no code execution, no calculator, nothing to run. This is a single response. The GitHub repository that accompanies the paper is RikiyaT/lm_understand-logs. The exp1_which_game folder contains the prompts they used, with weights for the four games: breakout/prompt.txt freeway/prompt.txt pacman/prompt.txt pong/prompt.txt I checked and, aside from the weights, each prompt is identical to the others. Trying it out with Qwen 3.8 27B and other models The prompts on GitHub are self-contained. My LLM tool has a -f/--fragment option which can be passed the URL to a text file to use as a prompt, which means I can run any of the above like this: llm -f https://raw.githubusercontent.com/RikiyaT/lm_understand-logs/refs/heads/main/exp1_which_game/runs/breakout/prompt.txt \ -m openrouter/moonshotai/kimi-k3.1-pro This example requires the llm-openrouter plugin installed, and runs the Breakout prompt against Kimi K3.1 Pro via OpenRouter. Here's what I got back from Kimi K3.1 Pro, which successfully guessed Breakout after 9m 41s and 19,493 reasoning tokens. But how about Qwen 3.8 27B? It's important that HTML in these stays tightly sandboxed to avoid untrusted code interacting with OpenAI APIs and accessing private data. Parameter found quite a complex attack chain. OpenAI use the MathLive JavaScript library as part of their graph support, and that library includes its own virtual keyboard, which can be triggered in the parent page via postMessage() from the sandboxed iframe. Three-Body Orbits

There are no generalized solutions to the three-body problem. Say you have three planets orbiting each other: it’s impossible to calculate how they will move using Newton’s laws of motion and gravity. Unlike the two-body problem, the three-body problem has no general closed-form analytic solution. The differential equations that govern the motions of three gravitating bodies are not integrable and cannot be solved to give explicit formulas for the positions of the bodies as a function of time. For most initial conditions, the dynamical system for three orbiting bodies is chaotic, and the only way to predict their motions is to estimate them using numerical methods. But thousands of stable three-body orbits have been found — their paths repeat after a given interval. The Three Body Orbits website is an atlas of some of those orbits: you can watch each three-body system play itself out in a wide variety of different shapes and complexities. When a user hits one, or Savi hits one in the product, she files it. It goes straight to an agent that decides whether it's a real bug to fix, something we won't fix, or intentional design that needs a decision. Usually it becomes a change, delivered to that user within an hour, with a message in the same chat. These are commonly referred to as gift links. Thousands of gift links are shared on websites and social media daily. Some helpful aggregated feeds and hashtags are available, but finding specific information can be difficult. Whenever possible here on KDO, I link to paywalled articles with gift links. To help in that endeavor, I’ve built a gift link discovery tool called Prometheus. Prometheus collects gift links to paywalled news articles shared by subscribers — from the New York Times, the Washington Post, the Atlantic, and more. Links are gathered from Bluesky, Mastodon, and the web throughout the day, and you can search by keyword & URL and filter by publication. Right now, Prometheus is only available to active kottke.org members while I’m testing and refining it — it’s definitely a beta tool rn. If you’re a member, check it out and let me know if you’ve got any feedback or notice any rough edges.
